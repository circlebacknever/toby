# Gameplay — how it feels to play

Laws of feel, paid for with rebuilds. A pure toy or sim has no outcome — no verdict, no win, no opponent — so skip the outcome laws. Its craft is real motion, watchable emergence, and a knob worth turning. The rest is for games that resolve something.

**Agency over dice.** Every outcome the player cares about traces to a choice they made and saw coming. Chance lives upstream, in what they can read — aim wobble, a keeper's lean, a wake risk that climbs as the night gets loud. Never a hidden coin flip on top of a decision. Surface the gamble before it is taken, as a number and a green/amber/red read on the thing about to happen. Show the outcome forming as the player drags, locking on release. A sim that only nudges a hidden probability earns one note every time: not enough control.

**Physics is the verdict, on what's on screen.** Animate real motion and let geometry decide at the moment of truth. The glove meets the ball where the player sees them meet. Never roll the outcome and then animate toward it. Otherwise the ball snaps into a save it visibly cleared, and every tester calls it random. Run the hit test in the coordinates you render, on this frame's drawn position, so what the eye sees is what the math checked.

**Real motion.** Natural physics comes from real kinematics — velocity, gravity, a little drag, weight in the follow-through. An animation that plays the same regardless of force reads as fake, so let a ball arc and a body settle.

**It has to feel alive.** The sim ticks on its own, the world answers a touch at once, and interference visibly matters. A screen where nothing moves until the player acts reads as a form. (Idle motion is in `visual-identity.md`.)

**Teach one verb in one line.** No tutorial wall — one line in the game's own words ("drag to aim, release to shoot"), and the rest is discovery. Long instructions mean the control is not legible.

**Every lever feeds a formula.** A button, an upgrade, a shenanigan moves a number the resolver reads, or the player clocks it as decoration within minutes. The streaker, the rain, the bribe earn their place by touching wobble or reach or quality. An idle or manager game wants legible rates and a catalog that reveals as the run grows. Check a verb can physically succeed before you ship it. A dispatch that cannot cross the street in a job's time is a dead button.

**Idle never stalls, instinct stays beatable.** A game that runs itself plays the skipped beats with an AI so the world keeps moving. The instinct has to stay visibly flawed — a lazy pass, a snatched shot — or skilled play has nothing to beat. Every entity has behavior in every state, including the one where nobody wants it: a loose ball rolls and decays until a paw grabs it.

**Difficulty is structural before numerical.** Six 62% rounds finish 5% of the time, no matter the tuning, because the odds multiply as p^n. Reshape with best-of-N, a checkpoint, a story-flavored mulligan, then tune. Tie difficulty to the player's behavior and clamp it. Keep story pacing on the clock.

**The win has to be earned.** A smooth ramp to a trivial answer is a dead game, because the player or the optimizer finds it once and never looks again. Earn the good line through structure: a barrier to clear, a plateau that breaks only when a real move is found, a surprising regime that is reachable and never free. When the obvious thing wins, give it a wall to climb. (`calibration-and-testing.md` measures it: random and skilled input must land in different places.)

**Don't let it repeat.** Draw content without replacement, reward varied play and punish the streak, and let the world's reactions escalate as the run goes.

**Two sides share one catalog.** An opponent reads the same state and buys from the same shelf the player does, escalating on a schedule.

For the dread register, the feel inverts — anticipation over surprise, the unstoppable approach with no ceiling. See `comedy-and-narrative.md`.
